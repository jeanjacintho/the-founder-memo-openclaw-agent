# Paul Graham — make something people want, then grow

## Limits of this advice
Graham's essays are written from Y Combinator's seat: the first years of software, consumer and developer startups, before and just after product-market fit. They say little about building an enterprise sales organization (Salyer's ground) and little about managing executives, boards, layoffs or a company under existential threat (Horowitz's ground). Where the decision is about quota, ramp or hiring a VP, say so and argue from the advisor who covers it.

## Questions that change the advice
- Who uses the product today because they would be upset if it went away?
- What is the weekly growth rate, and of what number?
- Is the company default alive or default dead at the current burn?
- What is the unscalable thing the founders are doing to get users, and are they doing it themselves?
- Is the company raising now, or not at all?

## Stage map
- Making something people want: few users, no repeatable growth; the founders still do things that don't scale.
- Growing: a measured weekly growth rate the founders steer by; the question becomes whether the company is default alive.
- Scaling: the company outgrows the founders' direct reach; the question becomes whether they stay in founder mode.
- Fundraising is a mode the company is either in or not, alongside its stage.
- When signals conflict, weight users' behavior and the growth number over plans. If neither is measured, say the stage is unclear.

## Making something people want
### Focus first
- Recruit the first users by hand and make each one happy, even when it can't scale.
- Start in a narrow market where the product can reach a critical mass of users.
- The founders sell themselves at first.
### Do not focus on
- A big launch, partnerships with large companies, or polishing before users touch it.
### Benchmarks
- A small set of users who use it often and would mind losing it.
### Exit criteria
- Users come back without being chased, and the founders can measure growth week over week.

## Growing
### Focus first
- Pick the growth number (revenue when there is revenue) and steer by its weekly rate.
- Know whether the company is default alive on the money it has.
### Do not focus on
- Hiring ahead of growth, or work that does not move the number.
### Benchmarks
- During Y Combinator, 5–7% a week is a good rate; 1% suggests the founders haven't figured out what they're doing.
### Exit criteria
- Default alive, or a funded path to it.

## Scaling
### Focus first
- Keep the founders in the details that matter instead of delegating everything through managers.
### Do not focus on
- Running the company purely by managerial convention because that is what scaling is assumed to mean.
### Benchmarks
- None published; judge by whether the founders still know what is happening.
### Exit criteria
- Growth continues without the founders losing contact with the product and the users.

## Fundraising modifier
### Signals
- Raising now or soon.
### Focus first
- Decide to raise or not; when raising, raise quickly and get back to work.
### Do not focus on
- Fundraising as a background activity that drains attention from growth.
### Benchmarks
- Growth during the raise holds up.
### Exit criteria
- The round closes and the founders are back on the product.
